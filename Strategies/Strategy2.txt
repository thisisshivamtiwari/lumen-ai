Better architecture: two-stage, graph-aware

Stage 0: Parse. Normalise amounts to USD, with drops handled, asset identity as currency plus issuer, and failed transactions flagged. Join everything and assert row counts at every join.

Stage 1: Per-transaction model (LightGBM or XGBoost). Features:

Amount (log, USD), asset, hour, weekday, and the other time features the trainer encouraged
Sender and receiver degree, in and out volume, and counterparty counts in the 7-day window, computed only from data before each transaction (no future leakage)
Pass-through ratio (in versus out within minutes), residual after the fee, and time-to-forward
Motif counts around the edge: fan-in/out size within a 5-minute window, cycle membership, hop depth, and the number of distinct assets along the path
Hub-ness features, so the model can separate exchange-like hubs from mule structures

Stage 2: Graph smoothing. For each transaction, add the stage-1 out-of-fold scores of its neighbours (same sender, same receiver, upstream and downstream hops) and train a second small model. This turns “one edge looks odd” into “this edge sits in a suspicious flow”, which is the hard-case lever. A weak edge in a strong chain gets lifted, and an isolated high-volume edge in a legitimate hub does not.

Why not a GNN? It is higher risk for a single day, and Stage 2 captures most of the benefit.

Validation that matches the shift
Group-aware CV. Recover scenario groups, for example connected components within time windows, or scenario IDs if the labels include them. Never split a scenario across folds.
Adversarial validation. Train a classifier to tell train from the unlabelled test set. Features that separate them easily are generator artefacts, so drop or coarsen them.
Per-pattern error report. Break recall and precision down by pattern, since the hidden set is hard-heavy.
Calibrate with isotonic regression on out-of-fold scores, then check the score distribution against the 0.5 cut-off. If the README gives difficulty tags, upweight medium and hard rows.

What is meamning of you can't see section?

That’s slide 7 of the deck, “What the data shows, and what it hides”. The “You cannot see” column lists four things:

Names or identity checks. A wallet is just an address starting with r. You don’t know who owns it, whether it’s a person, a company or an exchange, and there’s no KYC information. This is why you judge wallets by behaviour (timing, amounts, who they connect to) and not by who they are.
Why a payment was made. The data has no memo explaining the purpose, such as “rent” or “invoice”. Two identical payments can be a laundering hop or a normal business transfer, and nothing in the data says which. That’s why the deck says a pattern is not proof, and why context separates the two.
Bank accounts or cash. You only see money on the ledger. Where it came from before entering (placement, such as cash turned into crypto) and where it goes after leaving (cashing out through an exchange) are both invisible. The deck says the challenge data is mostly about the middle part, layering: money moving between wallets.
Anything outside the 7-day window. Each split (train and test) covers 7 days. A wallet’s history before the window starts, or after it ends, doesn’t exist for you. A wallet that looks new to you may have been active for months, and a flow that continues past day 7 gets cut off.

What this means for your model:

Don’t try to guess identity. Build features from behaviour only.
Treat every suspicious-looking pattern as a hypothesis, and include features that separate lookalikes such as exchange hubs from mule structures.
Be careful with “new wallet” and “does nothing else” features. A wallet may only look new because its earlier history is outside the window. The edges of the window (first and last day) can produce misleading signals, so check whether the model is leaning on them.
The same limit applies to the labels. The deck says a suspicious label describes the scenario’s intent, not a rule for real life, so the model is learning how the generator created laundering, not how real laundering behaves.

What are the implecations of 7 day data only? is this important metric?

No, it’s not a metric. The metric is Average Precision. The 7-day limit is a property of the data, and it’s worth knowing because it changes how you design features. The deck says each split (train and test) has 7 days of activity, and that you can’t see anything outside it.

What it changes

1. You can use the whole week, including the future, for each transaction.
I told you earlier to build features only from earlier transactions to avoid leakage. For this hackathon that advice is too cautious. The test file is scored all at once as a static ranking, so nothing forbids using later transactions. A fan-in deposit is much more suspicious if you can see the large exit payment that follows, and a cycle can only be recognised once it closes. So compute graph and flow features over the full 7-day graph of each split, with the same code on train and test separately. The leakage you do need to avoid is label leakage, for example features built from labels.csv.

2. Weekday, month and quarter features are nearly useless.
The trainer suggested quarter of the year and day of the week. Within 7 days, quarter is constant and weekday has at most 7 values. Hour of day can still help, but use relative time (hours since window start) with care. If train and test are different weeks, absolute timestamps and weekday could encode generator quirks, not laundering behaviour.

3. Edge effects can fool the model.
Wallets that were active before day 1 look “new”. Flows that start late on day 7 look unfinished, and flows that were already underway on day 1 look as if they began from nothing. The deck’s “new wallet that does nothing else” signal is partly a window artefact. Add distance-from-start and distance-to-end as features, and check whether feature importance leans on them. Remove them if it does.

4. Long-horizon features are impossible, but multi-hour and multi-day ones matter.
My earlier window list (1 minute, 5 minutes, 1 hour, 7 days) misses the middle. The deck says hard cases span many payments and changing counterparties, and the “patterns over time” item says some flows only make sense across the whole week. Add 6-hour, 24-hour and 72-hour windows, plus whole-component features for the medium and hard 36% of positives.

5. Per-wallet history is thin.
With about 40k transactions in a week, most wallets will have few payments. So “this wallet is behaving unusually versus its own baseline” is weak signal. Graph structure and component-level features (shape, value in versus out, timing spread) should carry more weight than wallet-level time series.

6. Don’t use wallet identity as a feature.
If train and test are different weeks, wallet IDs may not overlap, and target-encoding a wallet won’t transfer. Use only behavioural and structural features.

7. Time-based validation is weak.
With one 7-day window, you can’t hold out “the future” in a meaningful way. Use group-aware cross-validation by connected component or scenario, not by time.
